## 1. Engineering problem

Tuesday, 14:10. You have been stuck for four hours on a failing integration test. At standup yesterday you said "working on the report ticket, going fine". At 14:10 you post in the team channel: "hey, anyone around? having issues with tests". Nobody answers for 40 minutes because nobody knows what you need, and a reply requires a question back. Your lead eventually asks "which tests? what issue?", you reply with a screenshot of half a stack trace, and by 16:00 you have lost a day. On Thursday you open a PR titled "fixes" with an empty description. The reviewer spends 30 minutes working out what it is for, misses a defect, and a bug ships. In the retrospective, the estimate you gave ("two days") turns out to have been a guess you never flagged as one.

Specific failures of poor communication:

1. **Hidden blockers.** Problems surface late, when the cost to the team is highest.
2. **Unanswerable questions.** A question that requires a clarifying question costs two round trips, which across time zones can be a day.
3. **Unreviewable PRs.** Reviewers cannot see intent, risk, or test evidence, so they either skim (defects pass) or stall (delivery slows).
4. **Silent assumptions.** You build the wrong thing correctly because an ambiguous requirement was never clarified.
5. **Dishonest estimates.** A single number given under social pressure becomes a commitment; the miss damages trust more than the honest range would have.

Writing well is an engineering skill with checkable properties: completeness, self-containedness, brevity, and an explicit ask.

---

## 2. Learning objectives

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. Write a standup update in the done / next / blocked structure where every item is verifiable and no item is activity without outcome ("working on X").
2. Write a blocker message that a teammate can act on without asking you anything: context, what you tried, evidence, the specific ask, and what you will do meanwhile. Score at least 8 of 10 on the checklist in Section 3.3.
3. Rewrite a vague question into a self-contained one with a minimal reproduction, and decide with a stated rule whether to ask now or keep investigating.
4. Write a PR description with purpose, change summary, verification evidence, risk, and reviewer guidance, for a diff of your own.
5. Turn an ambiguous requirement into a short list of clarifying questions with proposed defaults, so the stakeholder can answer yes/no.
6. Produce an estimate as a range with stated assumptions, a confidence level, and the biggest unknown, and update it with a dated message when facts change.
7. Grade a piece of workplace writing against a 100-point rubric and name its two most important defects.

## 3. First-principles explanation

### 3.1 What communication is for, and why it fails

Teammates have limited attention and incomplete context. Each message is an interrupt that transfers context and requests (or gives) a decision. Good messages minimise the reader's cost: they can act in one read. A message fails when the reader must (a) guess what is wanted, (b) hunt for context, or (c) reply with a question before they can help.

Three principles drive everything below:

- **Self-contained.** The message makes sense without the thread, the call, or your screen. Link to evidence rather than describing it from memory.
- **Lead with the ask or the conclusion.** The first line says what you need or what happened. Details follow for those who need them.
- **Separate fact, inference, and request.** "The test fails with X (fact). I think Y is the cause because Z (inference). Could you confirm Y or point me at where config is set? (request)." This lets the reader correct your inference without redoing your investigation.

Tone: be direct and specific, not apologetic or dramatic. "Sorry to bother you, I'm probably missing something obvious, so stupid question" adds noise and no information. Honesty beats projecting confidence: "I don't know" followed by what you have checked is a strong sentence.

Choose the channel by urgency and permanence. Chat suits quick, ephemeral questions; tickets and PRs suit anything others must find later; a call suits misunderstandings that survived two written exchanges (and then write down the outcome). `ASSUMPTION:` your team uses chat plus tickets plus PRs; discover the actual norms in Section 3.9.

### 3.2 Standups and progress updates

A standup serves the team, not the manager: it surfaces dependencies and blockers early. The common structure is **done / next / blocked**, with each item stated as an outcome.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Worked on the report ticket." | "Reproduced the wrong total (test added, failing); cause is in `store.total`, fix in PR #212 awaiting review." |
| "Looking into the test failures." | "Two tests fail on clean main (baseline, see thread). I'm comparing CI env vs mine; will report by 11:00." |
| "No blockers." (while stuck) | "Blocked: need read access to the staging DB; asked in #infra at 09:20, no reply yet. Meanwhile continuing with local stub." |

Rules: name the ticket or PR; say what is now true that was not true yesterday; give a time for the next update if you are unsure; list blockers even if you are working around them; keep it to 30 seconds spoken or 5 lines written. A written daily update (end of day) has the same shape plus "tomorrow's first step", which also tells you where to resume. Do not report hours; report state.

Asynchronous format (chat, ready to paste):

```
Done: <outcome + link>
Next: <one or two concrete items; first step>
Blocked/risk: <specific, who/what unblocks, since when> (or "none, checked: <what>")
```

### 3.3 Blocker messages

A **blocker message** is for when you cannot proceed without someone else or something outside your control. Ten-point checklist (each worth one point in grading):

1. Subject/first line states the ask, not "quick question".
2. Context: what you are trying to achieve (ticket link).
3. Exact symptom with the error text (copied, not paraphrased).
4. Environment/version (branch, commit, Python/PHP version, OS) where relevant.
5. Steps to reproduce or a minimal reproduction (Lesson 0.3).
6. What you already tried and what each attempt showed.
7. Your current best hypothesis, labelled as such.
8. The specific request (who should do what, or which question to answer).
9. Urgency and impact (what is delayed, deadline if any), stated factually.
10. What you will do while waiting, and when you will check back.

Template:

```
Ask: Could someone tell me where `API_TOKEN` is meant to come from for local tests? (ticket ENG-123)
Context: running the baseline suite before touching ENG-123 (day-one setup).
Symptom: `make test` with API_TOKEN unset -> KeyError: 'API_TOKEN' (app/deps.py:5), 3 tests. Full output: <link/paste>.
Env: main @ a1b2c3d, Python 3.12.3, venv per README.
Tried: CI config sets `test-token`; with that value tests pass (6/6). README does not mention it.
Hypothesis: it is meant to be set locally and just undocumented. Is that right?
Impact: not blocked; I am proceeding with the CI value. Want to be sure I am not missing a shared secret store.
Next: I'll propose a README line in a separate PR once confirmed.
```

Notice what is absent: no apology, no screenshot of text, no "does anyone know". Notice the labelled hypothesis and the stated non-urgency, which respects the reader's time.

### 3.4 Asking good questions

Before asking, spend a bounded effort and record it: read the docs and relevant code, search the repository and tickets, reproduce, form a hypothesis. A rule of thumb (opinion): on day one, ask after roughly 30-60 minutes of real effort on a blocker; as you learn the team's preference, adjust. Ask sooner when the answer lives only in someone's head (access, priorities, history of a decision) or the block is on a person; ask later when the answer is in code or docs. Never sit stuck silently for a day.

Question types and their shape:

- **Factual lookup** ("where is X configured?"): one line plus where you looked.
- **Debugging** ("why does X fail?"): blocker template with a minimal reproduction.
- **Design/approach** ("should I do A or B?"): state the goal, the two options with one-line trade-offs, your leaning and why, and a deadline for when you will proceed on your leaning if no objection.
- **Priority/requirement** ("do you want X?"): see 3.6.

The most effective construction for a design question is the **default with veto**: "I plan to do A because ...; shout by 15:00 if you prefer B." It turns a blocking question into a non-blocking review. Use it only for reversible decisions.

Anti-patterns: "hello" with no content (wait for a reply before stating the question; avoid this); "it doesn't work"; a screenshot of text instead of copyable text; pasting 500 lines of log unfiltered; the same question in three channels; asking a person who is not the owner without saying why you chose them; secrets in the message (never paste tokens, passwords, or customer data; redact, and if you pasted one by mistake, say so and ask for rotation promptly).

### 3.5 PR descriptions

The reviewer's questions, in order: What is this for? What does it change? How do I know it works? What could go wrong? Where should I look hardest? The description answers them.

Template:

```
## Why
<The problem, in user/system terms. Link ticket.>

## What changed
<2-5 bullets. What, not how. Mention anything that looks odd and is deliberate.>

## How I verified
<Commands run and results. Tests added (name them). Manual check performed. Baseline vs after: "suite: 412 passed before, 413 after.">

## Risk and rollback
<What could this break? Which callers/data? How to revert (usually: revert this commit). Migrations? Config?>

## Notes for reviewers
<Where to focus; what I deliberately did NOT change; open questions; follow-ups I'll raise separately.>
```

Rules: the title states the change in the team's style (discover it: Lesson 0.2); describe behaviour, not files; call out a decision you made under ambiguity ("clamped negative totals to 0; if the intent is to reject them, say so and I'll change to a 422"); include screenshots or example output for visible changes; do not paste secrets; keep the PR small enough to review (Lesson 0.2); respond to review comments without defensiveness: answer the question, make the change or explain with evidence, and mark resolved only what you addressed. Reviews are about the code, and your reaction is part of the review.

Receiving review: a comment that seems wrong is usually a question about something unclear in your description or code. Verify before agreeing or disagreeing ("I checked, `find()` can return None here, so I added the guard" beats "good catch!" or "no").

### 3.6 Clarifying ambiguous requirements

Ambiguity is the norm. A ticket that says "Total can be negative" does not say what should happen instead. Building on a silent guess is the expensive path. The method:

1. List what is **stated**, what is **implied**, and what is **missing**.
2. For each missing item that changes the behaviour, write a question with a **proposed default**, so the answer can be "yes".
3. Group questions by importance; ask the few that change the design, not twenty.
4. Ask the owner (product owner, ticket author), in the ticket where the answer will be recorded.
5. State what you will proceed with if you hear nothing, when, and what it costs to change later.
6. Record the answer in the ticket or PR so the decision survives.

Example (from Lesson 0.1's negative total):

```
Re ENG-123 "Total can be negative". Before I change anything, two questions:
1. If discount > subtotal, should the total be 0 (clamp) or should the order be rejected with an error? I propose clamp to 0 as the smaller change.
2. Should the same rule apply to the export job, which uses a different code path (export.py:88)?
Unless you say otherwise by Wednesday noon I'll do (1) clamp and (2) leave export untouched and open a follow-up ticket.
```

### 3.7 Estimating honestly

An estimate is a statement of uncertainty, not a promise. A single number hides the distribution. Give:

- **A range** (optimistic to pessimistic), not a point. "2 to 5 days."
- **Assumptions** the range depends on ("environment works as documented; no schema change; review within a day").
- **Confidence** or a flag for what would change it.
- **The biggest unknown** and the cheapest way to reduce it ("I can answer the schema question with a 1-hour spike").
- **Calendar vs effort:** effort days are not calendar days; account for reviews, meetings, and waiting. Say which you mean.

Technique: break into pieces you can name, estimate each as a range, add the ranges (the sum of pessimistic values overstates the total risk, so do not report it as certain either; just report the range and the unknowns). Compare to similar past work from your failure log and dossier (Lessons 0.1 and 0.3) because you have actual time-lost data. In week one, your estimates are weak by construction; say so, and prefer small tasks with fast feedback.

Updating: when facts change, send a dated update early: "Revising ENG-123 from 2-3 days to 4-6: the export path duplicates the logic (found today). Cost of descoping the export: 1 day; your call." Early bad news is cheap; late bad news is not. Never silently slip.

Pressure: when someone asks "can you do it by Friday?", answer with what is true: "Friday is possible if X and Y hold; likelihood about half in my estimate (low confidence, first week on this codebase). If it must be Friday, the lowest-risk cut is Z." Saying no to a number is not saying no to the work.

### 3.8 Written clarity: a short editing method

1. Put the conclusion or ask first.
2. One topic per message; one purpose per paragraph.
3. Prefer concrete nouns and numbers ("3 of 6 tests", "since 09:20") over qualifiers ("some", "a while").
4. Cut preamble, apology, and hedges that carry no information.
5. Use lists for parallel items and code blocks for anything copyable.
6. Read it as the recipient, with no context, before sending. Could they act in one read?
7. Reduce emotion; state facts and impact. Incident-style messages follow the same shape and are covered with operations in Lesson 2.7.

### 3.9 Discovering the team's norms (week one)

Ask once, early, and write the answers down: where do questions go (channel, thread, person)? Expected response time? Standup format and time? Ticket workflow and who assigns work? PR size and review norms (who reviews, how fast, draft PRs)? How are blockers escalated? What is the working-hours and availability convention (important across time zones)? Observe: read the last ten merged PR descriptions, ticket comments, and channel history; match their length and style. If your team's norms differ from this lesson's templates, the team wins.

## 4. Worked example

Scenario: your first ticket, ENG-123, "Report total sometimes negative". You have followed Lesson 0.1 and reproduced it. Walk through the communication at each stage.

### 4.1 Standup, naive vs improved

Naive: "Yesterday I looked into the report thing. Today more of that. No blockers."

Improved:

```
Done: reproduced ENG-123 with a failing test (discount > subtotal gives -1.50); cause is store.total() having no lower bound.
Next: confirm intended behaviour with Priya (clamp vs reject), then fix + test. First step: ask in the ticket.
Blocked: no. Risk: export job uses separate logic (export.py:88), may need the same change; unknown scope.
```

What changed: outcomes instead of activity; a named next step; the unknown is surfaced as a risk instead of discovered during review.

### 4.2 Question, naive vs improved

Naive (14:10): "anyone around? having issues with tests".

Improved:

```
Could someone point me to how integration tests get a database locally? (ENG-123)
`pytest tests/integration` fails: `sqlalchemy.exc.OperationalError: connection refused (localhost:5432)`.
I read README and docker-compose.yml: compose defines `db` but `make dev` does not start it, and the README says nothing about integration tests.
I tried `docker compose up -d db` and the error became `password authentication failed for user "app"`, so I assume the password comes from a variable I do not have. Is that in a shared secret store, or is there a default for local use? Meanwhile I'm running only unit tests (all pass: 41/41).
```

What changed: the ask is first; the error is copied text; two things tried with results; a labelled assumption; the reader can answer in one line; no secret was pasted; the work continues in parallel.

### 4.3 Clarification and estimate

Clarification as in 3.6. Estimate message after the answer arrives:

```
ENG-123 estimate: 0.5-1 day for the web path (fix + test + PR), assuming clamp is the intended behaviour and review within a day. The export path (separate code) adds 0.5-2 days depending on whether it shares the calculation; I'll find out in a 1-hour spike tomorrow morning. Confidence: medium for the web path, low for export (I haven't read that code). I'll update after the spike.
```

### 4.4 PR description (filled in)

```
Title: fix: clamp order total at zero when discount exceeds subtotal (ENG-123)

## Why
Orders with a discount larger than the subtotal produced a negative total on the report page (ENG-123).

## What changed
- store.total() returns max(0, subtotal - discount).
- Added test_total_is_never_negative.
- Not changed: export.py has its own calculation (follow-up ENG-131).

## How I verified
- New test fails on main (AssertionError: -150 != 0) and passes on this branch.
- `make test`: 6 passed before, 7 after.
- Manual: GET /api/v1/orders/42 for the fixture with discount 250 returns total 0.

## Risk and rollback
Any caller that relied on negative totals (credit notes?) would change; I found none in `grep -rn "total("`, but I'm unsure about finance's spreadsheet export. Revert this commit to roll back.

## Notes for reviewers
Please check the credit-note question. If rejecting (422) is preferred, I'll change it; the ticket thread (link) agreed to clamp.
```

## 5. Production perspective

- **Maintainability.** PR descriptions and ticket comments are the project's memory: six months later `git blame` leads to a PR, and the description is the only record of why. Decisions recorded in writing survive staff changes.
- **Correctness.** Clarifying requirements before coding is a correctness activity; most expensive defects are correct implementations of the wrong requirement.
- **Security.** Never paste secrets, tokens, customer data, or production dumps into chat, tickets, PRs, or AI tools. Redact and describe. If you leak one, report it immediately and factually (what, where, when); rotation is the fix, and blame is irrelevant. Be wary of unexpected links or requests for credentials in chat; verify through a second channel. Ask before sharing internal details outside the company.
- **Failure handling / operations.** In an incident, communicate more, not less: state, impact, what you are doing, next update time. Lesson 2.7 covers incident and operations communication properly; the habit starts here.
- **Remote and cross-time-zone work.** Write for the person who reads at the start of their day: complete context, explicit ask, and a default that lets them proceed. Avoid "can we hop on a quick call?" as an opener; propose it after a written summary.
- **Testing.** Include in every PR how you tested, because "tests pass" is evidence only if the reviewer can see which tests.

**What a senior engineer does differently.** Posts blockers early with evidence; keeps messages short but complete; offers a default and a deadline; flags risk before being asked; separates what they know from what they believe; revises estimates openly; writes PR descriptions for the reviewer and the future reader; thanks reviewers briefly and acts on feedback; never uses communication to assign blame.

**Beginner mistakes and diagnosis.**

| Mistake | How you notice | Diagnosis and fix |
|---|---|---|
| Silent struggle | You have been stuck over two hours without telling anyone | Set a timer at the start of work on a blocker; post the blocker template when it fires |
| "Quick question" with no question | Replies are "what's up?" | Rewrite so the ask is the first line |
| Screenshot of text | Teammates ask for the text | Paste as code block; link logs |
| One giant PR | Review stalls | Split by purpose (Lesson 0.2) |
| Single-point estimates | You are surprised by misses | Always give a range plus assumptions |
| Over-apologising or over-confident tone | Messages are long or defensive | Use fact/inference/request structure |
| Asking the same question twice | Teammates sigh | Keep a notes file of answers (the dossier) |
| Defensive replies in review | Threads grow | Answer the question, show evidence, act |

## 6. Failure laboratory

Here is a real-style message sent by a new engineer at 16:55 on a Friday to a channel of 40 people:

> hey guys, sorry to bother, prod is acting weird?? the report page showed 0 for a customer and i think my change might have done it but not sure, maybe its the cache?? can someone look. also i pasted the db url below in case its useful: postgres://app:Tr0ub4dor@db-prod.internal/reports  [screenshot]. i'll be offline until monday, thanks!!

**Predict** before reading on. List every defect in this message (aim for at least eight), rank the three most serious, and write the order of actions the engineer should have taken instead.

### Reveal (read after predicting)

Defects: (1) credentials pasted in a 40-person channel: the most serious, since the password is now exposed and must be rotated; (2) the engineer announces they will be offline immediately after raising a possible production incident; (3) no ask directed at a person or a role (who should look?); (4) no evidence: no URL, no customer id, no time, no error text, and a screenshot instead of text; (5) cause is speculation ("maybe its the cache") not labelled with the evidence for or against; (6) the claim "my change might have done it" has no link to the change or deploy time; (7) vague impact: "acting weird", unknown scope (one customer or many); (8) wrong channel for an incident (team chat instead of the on-call route, if there is one, `ASSUMPTION`); (9) tone is apologetic and unstructured; (10) no next-update time.

Top three, in order: credential exposure; offline immediately after reporting a production issue; no evidence or impact. Correct sequence: (a) check the facts you can in two minutes (is it one customer, since when, does it correlate with your deploy?), without touching production data; (b) use the team's incident or on-call route, whatever it is, and say clearly "possible production issue, caused by my deploy of <PR> at <time>"; (c) state facts and impact; (d) offer the revert (`git revert <hash>` of the PR is the likely rollback, but the decision belongs to the owner) and stay reachable until someone owns the issue; (e) if you pasted a secret, say so at once and ask for rotation, then delete the message only after notifying (deleting does not undo exposure); (f) update at a stated time.

A better message:

```
Possible prod issue, may be caused by my change. Who is on call for reports?
Fact: the report for customer 8841 shows total 0 since ~16:30 (link). I don't know yet whether other customers are affected.
My change: PR #212 (clamp total at 0) deployed at 16:20. Possible link: that customer has a discount > subtotal. I have not verified the cause.
Proposal: revert #212 (one commit). I'm online and will do it on your go-ahead, or tell me who decides.
Note: I accidentally posted a DB connection string in my previous message; it needs rotating. Removing the message now.
Next update at 17:15.
```

Diagnosis procedure for reviewing any message: read as the recipient; answer "what is wanted from me, by when?"; scan for secrets; list facts vs guesses; check that the sender will be reachable.

## 7. Retrieval practice

Closed-book; answers in the solutions file.

1. Give the three parts of the done / next / blocked standup and rewrite "worked on the API" as an outcome.
2. Why separate fact, inference, and request in a blocker message?
3. List six of the ten blocker-message checklist items.
4. When should you ask a question immediately instead of continuing to investigate? Give two conditions.
5. What is the "default with veto" technique and when is it inappropriate?
6. A PR description needs five sections. Name them and say which one reviewers most often miss.
7. A ticket says "make the export faster". Write three clarifying questions, each with a proposed default.
8. Why is "2 days" a poor estimate? Rewrite it as a good one for a task whose unknown is a schema change.
9. You discover on day 3 that a 3-day task will take 6. Write the update message in four lines.
10. A reviewer comment seems wrong. Describe the steps before replying.
11. You pasted an API token into a team channel. What do you do, in order?
12. Name three things to find out about team communication norms in week one.

## 8. Independent implementation challenge

1. A blocker message (maximum 150 words) for this situation: after following the README, `docker compose up` exits with `port is already allocated` for 5432; you have a local PostgreSQL installed natively; the README does not mention it. 4. An estimate for a task you completed in this book (for example the 0.1 `repo_map.py` tool), written as you would have before starting: range, assumptions, confidence, biggest unknown. Then compare with your actual time and record the ratio in your failure log (it is data, not a failure).

Done when: each piece scores at least 8/10 on the checklist or rubric relevant to it, as graded by yourself and then by a reviewer who has not seen your intentions.

## 10. Engineering review

Self-review checklist for every written item: the first line is the ask or conclusion; a stranger could act on it; facts are separated from inferences; evidence is linked or pasted as text; no secrets or personal data; the ask names who should act and by when; impact and urgency are stated factually; the tone is neutral; the length is the minimum that is complete; the next update time is stated if work continues.

Explain your decisions: why did you choose this channel for each message? For each estimate, which assumption would you challenge first? For each default-and-veto, what makes the decision reversible? Which message could you shorten by half without losing information, and what would you cut?

## 11. Mastery gate

Pass = 80/100 and no critical failure. Critical failures: (1) any real or realistic secret, token, password, or customer data in the kit; (2) a blocker message with no specific ask; (3) an estimate given as a single number with no assumptions; (4) a PR description with no verification evidence; (5) blame language aimed at a person.

Evidence: the kit; the graded sample; a 10-minute live exercise in which a reviewer hands you a vague situation (a failing build, an unclear ticket) and you write a blocker message and a clarification in the time allowed, closed-book.

If failed: the remedial exercise is three new scenarios from the reviewer. For each, write the message in 10 minutes. The reviewer grades with the checklist and returns only the lowest-scoring item. Revise once. Repeat until the average is at least 8/10 with no critical failure.
