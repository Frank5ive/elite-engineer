## 1. Engineering problem

A researcher tests 20 candidate signals on pure noise and reports the best one with p = 0.03. A model of "price vs volume" fits two random walks with R^2 = 0.24 and t-statistics above 1.96 in most trials. A Monte Carlo pricer reports 1.7183 with no error bar, and a colleague reruns it with another seed and gets 1.7211. Each is a statistical error, not a coding error: the code may be perfectly correct and the conclusion still false.

Measured on simulated data in this lesson: testing 20 independent null hypotheses at 5% finds at least one "significant" result 65% of the time (theory 64.2%); regressing one independent random walk on another yields |t| > 1.96 in 85.7% of trials; and a naive 95% confidence interval for the mean of an autocorrelated series covers the truth only 74.8% of the time.

This lesson gives you estimation with honest uncertainty, the machinery of testing and its abuse, regression derived from first principles, the vocabulary of time series (stationarity, autocorrelation, random walks), and Monte Carlo with variance reduction, with reproducible random numbers.

## 3. First-principles explanation

### 3.1 Estimation, standard error, confidence intervals

An **estimator** is a rule turning a sample into a number; the estimate varies from sample to sample, and its spread is the **standard error** (SE). For the mean of n independent draws, SE = s/sqrt(n) with s the sample standard deviation (divide by n-1, "Bessel's correction", so that the sample variance is unbiased: E[s^2] = sigma^2).

A **95% confidence interval** is a procedure: xbar +- t_{0.975, n-1} * SE. Its meaning is about the procedure, not this interval: if you repeated the whole experiment many times, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true mean. It does not say there is a 95% probability the parameter lies in a given computed interval.

Measured (20,000 experiments, true mean 10, sigma 2): n = 30, t critical value 2.0452, t-interval coverage 94.95%, z (1.96) coverage 94.03%. At n = 5 the z-interval covers only 88.1% while the t-interval (critical value 2.7764) covers 94.99%. The t distribution accounts for the extra uncertainty from estimating sigma.

Bias vs variance of an estimator, consistency (converges as n grows), and efficiency are the properties to ask of any estimator. Fails when: samples are dependent (3.7), data are heavy-tailed so the CLT is slow (6.1), or the sample is not representative.

### 3.2 Hypothesis testing

Null hypothesis H0 (e.g. mean = 0). Test statistic t = (xbar - 0)/SE. The **p-value** is the probability, assuming H0 and the model are true, of a statistic at least as extreme as observed. Example: n = 30, xbar = 0.5, s = 2: t = 1.369, two-sided p = 0.181 with 29 degrees of freedom - not significant.

What a p-value is not: not the probability H0 is true (that needs a prior - this is the Bayes base-rate problem of 6.1); not the effect size; not the chance the result replicates. **Power** is the probability of rejecting when the effect is real. For a true mean 0.5, sigma 2, n = 30, simulated power at 5% is 26.8%: the test would miss a real effect almost three times in four. Reporting "not significant" from an underpowered test says nearly nothing.

### 3.3 Abuse: multiple comparisons and p-hacking

Test k independent true nulls at level alpha: P(at least one false positive) = 1 - (1 - alpha)^k. For k = 20, alpha = 0.05: 64.2%. Simulated (5,000 repetitions of 20 t-tests on N(0,1) samples of size 30): 65.1%. **Bonferroni** tests each at alpha/k: family-wise error 1 - (1 - 0.0025)^20 = 4.88%; simulated 4.86%. It is conservative (loses power), the price of honesty; alternatives are Holm and false-discovery-rate control (names; derive from the references if needed).

p-hacking is any flexible procedure that tries many analyses (signals, parameters, date ranges, outlier rules) and reports the best. The fix is procedural: pre-register the number of trials, report all of them, correct for the count, and hold out data that was never used for selection. In quant work every parameter you tuned counts as a test.

### 3.4 Linear regression from the normal equations

Model: y = X beta + e, X is n x p (first column all ones for an intercept). Choose beta to minimize the sum of squared residuals S(beta) = (y - X beta)^T (y - X beta). Set the gradient to zero:

    dS/dbeta = -2 X^T (y - X beta) = 0   =>   X^T X beta = X^T y     (normal equations)

so beta_hat = (X^T X)^-1 X^T y, computed by solving the linear system (6.2), never by explicit inverse. Geometric meaning: the residual r = y - X beta_hat is orthogonal to every column of X (X^T r = 0; measured max |X^T r| = 6e-14, rounding noise), i.e. the fit is the orthogonal projection of y onto the column space.

Uncertainty: with iid errors of variance sigma^2, Cov(beta_hat) = sigma^2 (X^T X)^-1, estimated with s^2 = RSS/(n - p). R^2 = 1 - RSS/TSS is the fraction of variance explained.

Worked run (n = 200, y = 2 + 3x + noise(sd 0.5), x uniform on [-1,1], seed 12345): beta_hat = (1.9724, 2.9394), SE = (0.0322, 0.0547), s = 0.4549, R^2 = 0.9358; `lstsq` and `scipy.stats.linregress` agree to the digits shown. The truth (2, 3) lies within 1 SE and 1.1 SE.

Failure modes: (a) collinear predictors - with two columns differing by 1e-7 noise, cond(X) = 2.0e7 but cond(X^T X) = 4.0e14 (the square), so normal equations square the condition number; production code uses QR or SVD (`lstsq`). (b) Overfitting: with 30 points from a straight line (true R^2 about 0.5), polynomial degree 1, 3, 9, 15 gave in-sample R^2 0.399, 0.427, 0.525, 0.592 but out-of-sample (10,000 fresh points) 0.469, 0.448, 0.442, 0.069. In-sample R^2 never decreases when you add terms; only out-of-sample performance is evidence. (c) Errors that are autocorrelated or heteroskedastic make the SE formula wrong. (d) Regressing non-stationary series (3.7).

### 3.5 Stationarity and autocorrelation

A series is (weakly) **stationary** if its mean and variance are constant over time and the covariance between x_t and x_{t+k} depends only on the lag k. Most statistics you compute (means, correlations, regressions) assume this; prices are not stationary, returns approximately are (6.4).

Sample autocorrelation at lag k: r_k = sum_{t}(x_t - xbar)(x_{t+k} - xbar) / sum_t (x_t - xbar)^2. For white noise r_k is near 0 with SE about 1/sqrt(n).

AR(1): x_t = phi x_{t-1} + e_t with |phi| < 1 is stationary, variance 1/(1 - phi^2) (for unit-variance e), autocorrelation phi^k. Simulated phi = 0.5, n = 100,000: r_1, r_2, r_3 = 0.497, 0.245, 0.120 (theory 0.5, 0.25, 0.125); variance 1.329 (theory 1.333).

Consequence for inference: with positive autocorrelation, observations carry less independent information. The variance of the mean of an AR(1) series is about (sigma_x^2/n)(1+phi)/(1-phi): for phi = 0.5 that is three times the naive value. Measured (n = 1000, 4,000 series): actual variance of the mean 0.00387 versus naive 0.00133 (theory 0.00400); the naive 95% interval covered the truth in 74.8% of series. The "effective sample size" is n(1-phi)/(1+phi) = n/3. Daily returns with volatility clustering and serially correlated signals violate iid in this way.

### 3.6 Random walks and Brownian motion

Random walk: S_t = S_{t-1} + e_t. It is not stationary: Var(S_t) = t sigma^2 grows without bound. Simulated 100,000 steps: autocorrelations of the levels r_1, r_10, r_100 = 0.9999, 0.9991, 0.9913 (misleadingly high; any trending series looks that way), but of the increments -0.0017 and -0.0002 (near 0, as for white noise). Diagnostic: difference the series and look again.

**Spurious regression**: regress one independent random walk (250 steps) on another: |t| > 1.96 in 85.7% of 2,000 trials, mean R^2 = 0.240. Regress their differences (independent white noise): 5.8% reject, close to the nominal 5%. The t-statistic's null distribution assumes stationarity, so it fails on levels. Test relationships in returns (differences), or use methods designed for cointegration (beyond this volume).

**Brownian motion discretization**: W_0 = 0, W_{t+dt} = W_t + sqrt(dt) Z, Z ~ N(0,1) independent. Increments are independent with variance dt, hence Var(W_t) = t. Simulated 100,000 paths, 252 steps over [0,1]: Var(W_1) = 0.9993, mean -0.0001; Var(W_0.5) = 0.5014. Geometric Brownian motion S_T = S_0 exp((mu - sigma^2/2) T + sigma W_T) has E[S_T] = S_0 e^{mu T}; with S_0 = 1, mu = 0.1, sigma = 0.2, T = 1: sample mean 1.10514 (SE 0.00071) vs exact 1.10517. The -sigma^2/2 term is the Ito correction (6.4); omit it and the expectation is wrong. Discretization of a path introduces error that shrinks with finer time steps; for this exact solution, one step is already exact in distribution, which is why you should not time-step when a closed form exists.

### 3.7 Monte Carlo: error vs N

To estimate I = E[f(X)], average f over N iid draws; the error is distributed with standard deviation sigma_f/sqrt(N), independent of dimension. That is the whole appeal: slow (halving error costs 4x samples) but dimension-free.

Test problem with a known answer: I = E[exp(U)], U uniform(0,1) = e - 1 = 1.71828. Var(exp U) = (e^2 - 1)/2 - (e-1)^2 = 0.24204. Predicted RMSE at N: sqrt(0.24204/N). Measured RMSE over 400 independent repetitions each: N = 100: 4.83e-2 (theory 4.92e-2); 1,000: 1.51e-2 (1.56e-2); 10,000: 4.78e-3 (4.92e-3); 100,000: 1.55e-3 (1.56e-3); 1,000,000: 5.08e-4 (4.92e-4). Log-log slope fitted: -0.495 (theory -0.5). Plot or tabulate RMSE against N on log axes; a slope far from -0.5 signals a bug, dependence, or an infinite-variance integrand.

A single run's 95% CI xbar +- 1.96 SE (SE from the sample standard deviation) covered the truth in 95.2% of 4,000 runs at N = 1000.

### 3.8 Variance reduction: antithetic variates

If U is uniform then 1-U is too, and for monotone f, f(U) and f(1-U) are negatively correlated. Average the pair: Y = (f(U) + f(1-U))/2. Var(Y) = (Var f(U) + Cov(f(U), f(1-U)))/2. For exp: Cov = E[e^U e^(1-U)] - (e-1)^2 = e - (e-1)^2 = -0.23421, so Var(Y) = (0.24204 - 0.23421)/2 = 0.003912.

Fair comparison uses equal cost, i.e. equal number of function evaluations: N evaluations buy N/2 pairs, so variance of the antithetic mean is Var(Y)/(N/2) = 0.007825/N versus 0.24204/N: a variance reduction of 30.9x (RMSE ratio 5.6x). Measured RMSE ratio plain/antithetic at equal evaluations: 5.6, 5.2, 5.4, 5.5, 5.8 for N = 10^2..10^6 (400 repetitions each; RMSE estimates carry about 3.5% sampling error), consistent with the predicted sqrt(30.9) = 5.56. The log-log slope is still -0.5: variance reduction changes the constant, not the rate. Comparing at equal N pairs instead of equal evaluations would overstate the gain by 2x - a standard way to cheat.

Limits: antithetics help only for monotone-like integrands. For f(u) = (u - 0.5)^2, which is symmetric about 0.5, f(U) = f(1-U) exactly, so pairing doubles the cost for no reduction: measured variance of the antithetic estimator at equal cost was 2.07x worse than plain. Always test on your problem. Other techniques (control variates, importance sampling, stratification) are named here; implement them in the extension.

### 3.9 Random number generators in practice

Pseudo-random generators are deterministic given a seed. Rules: (1) seed explicitly and log the seed; (2) use `numpy.random.default_rng(seed)` (a `Generator`), not the legacy global `np.random.seed` state; (3) for parallel or repeated runs derive independent streams with `np.random.SeedSequence(seed).spawn(k)` rather than seeds 1, 2, 3 by hand (adjacent integer seeds are usually fine with modern generators but spawn gives a documented guarantee - VERIFY against the numpy docs on parallel generation); (4) same seed gave identical arrays in my run (`default_rng(7).random(3)` twice), and spawned children gave different streams; (5) reproducibility holds for a fixed numpy version and platform, not forever; (6) never use a PRNG for security; (7) reusing one seed across "independent" replications gives identical results (6.1's failure laboratory).

## 4. Worked example

Goal: estimate E[exp(U)] with an honest error bar, a method comparison, and an error-vs-N table. Design choices: (a) one function per method taking `n` and a `Generator`, so cost is explicit; (b) replications get children of one `SeedSequence`, so the whole experiment is reproducible from one integer; (c) compare at equal function evaluations; (d) compare to a known analytic answer.

```python
# mcexp.py - Python 3.12+, numpy>=1.26
import math, numpy as np
TRUE = math.e - 1

def plain(n, rng):            # n function evaluations
    return np.exp(rng.random(n)).mean()

def antithetic(n, rng):       # n function evaluations = n/2 pairs
    u = rng.random(n // 2)
    return ((np.exp(u) + np.exp(1 - u)) / 2).mean()

def rmse(method, n, reps, seed):
    children = np.random.SeedSequence(seed).spawn(reps)
    errs = np.array([method(n, np.random.default_rng(c)) - TRUE for c in children])
    return math.sqrt(np.mean(errs ** 2))

if __name__ == "__main__":
    xs, ys = [], []
    for k in range(2, 7):
        n = 10 ** k
        a, b = rmse(plain, n, 400, 1), rmse(antithetic, n, 400, 2)
        th = math.sqrt(((math.e**2 - 1) / 2 - TRUE**2) / n)
        print(n, f"{a:.3e} {b:.3e} {th:.3e} {a/b:.1f}")
        xs.append(math.log(n)); ys.append(math.log(a))
    print("slope", np.polyfit(xs, ys, 1)[0])
```

Output on the authoring machine: the table of section 3.7/3.8 and `slope -0.4947`. Note the two methods use different seeds (1 and 2); using the same seed would be acceptable too, and would show the same-stream comparison; the differing seeds avoid accidentally correlating the comparison.

Regression snippet (normal equations plus SE, cross-checked):

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(12345)
n = 200; x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, n); y = 2 + 3 * x + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
beta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
r = y - X @ beta; s2 = r @ r / (n - 2)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(s2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)))   # inv only for the small p x p covariance
assert np.allclose(beta, np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0], atol=1e-10)
```

Naive vs improved: a naive experiment prints one estimate from one seed. The improved version reports (i) SE from the sample, (ii) repeated-run RMSE, (iii) slope, (iv) equal-cost comparison. What changed and why: a single number cannot distinguish a correct estimator from a lucky one, and cannot show a bug that biases the estimate by less than one SE.

## 5. Production perspective

**Correctness.** Separate: estimator correct (unbiased/consistent), assumptions satisfied (independence, stationarity), implementation correct (tests against analytic answers), and conclusion supported (multiplicity counted). Tests: known-answer Monte Carlo with tolerance 4 SE, coverage tests (95% intervals should cover about 95% in simulation, with tolerance from binomial SE sqrt(0.95*0.05/reps)), slope test within +-0.1 of -0.5.

**Performance.** Vectorize (the whole N in one numpy call); memory for 10^8 draws is 800 MB per float64 array, so chunk and accumulate sums (use Welford or chunked means). Measure; I quote no timings. Variance reduction often beats raw speed (30.9x variance reduction is worth more than a 10x faster loop, which gives only 3.16x lower error).

**Failure handling and operations.** Log seed, N, library versions, estimate, SE, and wall time. Reject NaN draws loudly. For regression, check cond(X) and use `lstsq`/QR in production.

**What a senior engineer does differently.** Counts every trial that went into a result; holds out data before looking; reports intervals and effect sizes alongside p-values; differences non-stationary series before regressing; checks the log-log slope; compares variance reduction at equal cost; keeps the analytic oracle in tests.

**Common beginner mistakes and diagnosis.**

- Reporting the best of many tests: ask "how many things did you try?"; simulate the null with the same search and see how often you "win".
- Regressing price levels on price levels: check residual autocorrelation; difference and repeat.
- Calling a z-interval at small n: simulate coverage.
- Treating p = 0.04 as 96% certainty: restate as the definition.
- Reusing one RNG seed for every replication or every worker: compare outputs across replications for exact equality.
- Using in-sample R^2 to pick the model: split the data.

## 6. Failure laboratory

A colleague presents a "signal discovery" script:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
returns = rng.normal(0, 1, (500, 40))          # 500 days, 40 candidate signals, all pure noise
target = rng.normal(0, 1, 500)                 # next-day return, independent of everything
best = min(stats.pearsonr(returns[:, j], target).pvalue for j in range(40))
print("best p-value:", best)
```

He concludes "signal found at p < 0.05, ship it." **Predict first:** (a) roughly how often does this script print a best p-value under 0.05 across many different seeds? (b) What is the expected value of the smallest of 40 independent uniform p-values? (c) What single change in the analysis restores validity? (d) Why would a held-out period expose the problem?

### Reveal (read after predicting)

Under the null each p-value is uniform(0,1). P(best < 0.05) = 1 - 0.95^40 = 87.1% (the pearsonr tests are nearly independent here). The expected minimum of 40 uniforms is 1/41 = 0.024, so a typical run has a "significant" winner by construction. Correct for the 40 looks: Bonferroni threshold 0.05/40 = 0.00125 (family-wise error about 4.9%). Hold out data: apply only the chosen signal to a fresh period, where its p-value is again uniform under the null, so about 5% of noise signals pass, and the 87% inflation disappears.

Diagnosis procedure: (1) count the number of hypotheses explored, including informal ones; (2) simulate the entire pipeline on data with the effect removed (shuffle the target) and see how often it "finds" something; (3) apply the correction and see if the result survives; (4) test out of sample with the selection frozen; (5) report effect size and interval, not only p.

Second lab, for the time series: regress the cumulative sum of one noise series on another. Predict the typical |t| and R^2, then run it (section 3.6 reports 85.7% rejection, mean R^2 0.24).

## 10. Engineering review

Checklist: replications use spawned independent streams; tolerances derive from SE or binomial SE, never from guesses; equal-cost comparisons; slopes fitted on log-log scale with at least 5 points; analytic oracles retained; seeds and versions logged; multiplicity counted in any claim of "found"; time series differenced or shown stationary before regression; no use of in-sample fit as evidence.

Explain your design decisions: why `SeedSequence.spawn` rather than seeds 1..N; why RMSE and not mean error across replications (the mean error hides spread); why the Brownian maximum needs a discretization caveat (the discrete maximum underestimates the continuous one); what you would do if replications were expensive.

## 11. Mastery gate

Pass = 80/100 with no critical failure.

Critical failures: reporting an estimate without uncertainty; reusing a seed across replications; claiming variance reduction from an unequal-cost comparison; reporting the best of several tests without correction; regressing non-stationary levels and claiming a relationship; p-value described as the probability the null is true; unsupported performance claims.

Required evidence: D1-D7 pages; passing tests; the report with log-log table and slope; a 10-minute defense deriving the normal equations and the antithetic variance.

If failed: remedial exercise: for the weakest area write a 40-line simulation that shows the failure and the fix with numbers (coverage failure at n = 5; multiple-testing inflation; spurious regression; unequal-cost antithetic claim) and pin the results in tests.
