## 1. Engineering problem

You are asked: "Customers should be able to export their invoices." Three weeks later the export exists, nobody agrees what it was supposed to do, finance wants a format that was never mentioned, the nightly job times out for the largest customer, and a security review asks where the files are stored and for how long. When something breaks, the team spends a day reconstructing why it was built that way, because the reasoning lived in a chat thread.

Poor handling looks like: coding before the question is understood; decisions made implicitly and rediscovered; estimates given as a single optimistic number; incident reviews that name a person instead of a cause; performance claims with no method; migrations with no rollback.

The skill: turn ambiguity into explicit questions, assumptions and decisions; make reasoning reviewable by someone who was not in the room; estimate honestly with ranges; learn from failures without blame; and defend your work orally under questioning. Everything in this lesson is graded on **reasoning quality**, not on prose polish or length.

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Turn a vague requirement into a list of clarifying questions, explicit assumptions, non-goals and acceptance criteria, ranked by cost of being wrong.
2. Write a technical design proposal (max 4 pages) with alternatives considered, trade-offs, risks, test and rollout plan, which a reviewer can critique without asking you questions.
3. Write an ADR with context, decision, status, consequences and revisit conditions; supersede one correctly.
4. Produce a three-point estimate with explicit assumptions, compute expected value and spread by running code, and state what the estimate does not cover.
5. Run a root-cause analysis (timeline, contributing factors, causal chain, verification of the root cause) and write a blameless postmortem with actions that are specific, owned and checkable.
6. Write a performance report and a security review containing method, evidence, limits and a decision, not just numbers or checklists.
7. Write a migration plan with phases, verification, rollback and a point of no return.
8. Defend a design in a timed oral format, answering challenges with evidence or an honest "I do not know, here is how I would find out".

## 3. First-principles explanation

### 3.1 Clarifying ambiguous requirements

A requirement is ambiguous when two reasonable engineers would build different things. Method:

1. **Restate** in your own words as observable behaviour: "A logged-in customer can download a file containing all of their invoices for a chosen period."
2. **List unknowns** in categories: who (roles, permissions), what (fields, formats, volume), when (latency, frequency, retention), where (storage, regions, systems), why (decision this serves), constraints (cost, compliance, deadline), non-goals.
3. **Rank by cost of being wrong**: a wrong guess about the file format costs a week; a wrong guess about who may see whose invoices is a data breach. Ask the expensive questions first.
4. **Propose defaults**: "Unless told otherwise I will assume CSV, UTF-8, up to 100k rows, last 24 months, own-customer data only." A question with a proposed default is easier to answer than an open one.
5. **Write assumptions down** with an owner and a date to confirm. An unconfirmed assumption is a risk (7.1, register of unproven knowledge).
6. **Acceptance criteria** as testable statements: "Export of 100k invoices completes within the agreed time on the staging dataset"; "A user cannot export another customer's invoices (tested)". Time numbers come from the requester or from measured baselines, not from you guessing.
7. **Non-goals** stated: "No PDF rendering in this phase."

Failure modes: so many questions you stall (top 5-8 blockers, defaults for the rest); asking what the codebase already answers; hiding assumptions.

### 3.2 The technical design proposal

Purpose: get disagreement while it is cheap. Not documentation after the fact.

Template (target length 2-4 pages; delete empty sections rather than fill them with filler):

```
Title / author / reviewers / status (Draft | In review | Accepted | Superseded) / date
1. Context and problem       (why now; who is affected; link to requirement)
2. Goals and non-goals       (measurable where possible)
3. Assumptions and open questions (owner, due date)
4. Proposed design           (diagram, data model, interfaces, failure behaviour)
5. Alternatives considered   (at least two real ones, incl. "do nothing"; why rejected)
6. Trade-offs and risks      (what we give up; what could go wrong; mitigations)
7. Security and privacy      (data classes, access control, retention)
8. Testing and verification  (how we will know it works; what is not tested)
9. Rollout and rollback      (flags, phases, monitoring, abort criteria)
10. Operations               (metrics, alerts, runbook, cost)
11. Estimate and milestones  (ranges; see 3.4)
12. Decisions needed         (explicit asks of the reviewers)
```

Quality properties: a reviewer can find the weakest assumption in under five minutes; alternatives are strong (not strawmen); every risk has a mitigation or an explicit acceptance; measurable goals; consistent with team standards and existing architecture (never redesign what you do not own without the owners). The simplest design that meets the requirements wins by default (Vol 4). Complexity needs a stated requirement that forces it.

### 3.3 Architecture Decision Records

An ADR captures one decision so that a future engineer knows why. Short (half a page), immutable once accepted (supersede, do not edit history).

```
ADR-NNN: <decision as a short noun phrase>
Status: Proposed | Accepted | Deprecated | Superseded by ADR-MMM
Date:
Context:       forces at play, requirements, constraints (facts, not advocacy)
Decision:      "We will ..." (one decision)
Alternatives:  each with the reason it lost
Consequences:  positive, negative, neutral; what becomes harder
Revisit when:  explicit trigger (volume > X, vendor change, requirement Y)
```

A good ADR makes the losing alternatives look reasonable (they were) and names the specific condition under which you would choose differently. A bad ADR records a conclusion with no forces, or is written after the fact as justification. Write ADRs for decisions that are costly to reverse or that people will later question: storage engine, queue vs polling, sync vs async API, auth mechanism, schema ownership. Not for variable names.

### 3.4 Estimation

Estimates are forecasts with uncertainty, not commitments. Techniques:

- **Decompose** until each piece is a day or less of uncertain work; include testing, review, deployment, documentation, and integration; add explicit lines for unknowns you have identified.
- **Three-point**: for each task, optimistic O, most-likely M, pessimistic P. A common approximation (PERT) is expected `E = (O + 4M + P) / 6` and standard deviation `s = (P - O) / 6`. It is a convention with simplifying assumptions, not a law.
- **Aggregate**: expected values add. Variances add only if the tasks are independent, which they rarely are (the same unknown API hurts three tasks). So the aggregate spread from the formula is a lower bound on real uncertainty.
- **Reference classes**: compare with how long similar work took you or the team; record actuals to calibrate (the post-implementation review feeds this).
- **State what is excluded** (waiting on reviews, other priorities, production incidents) and the confidence level. Give a range and a date by which you will re-estimate.

Worked numbers (illustrative tasks and numbers I made up for this lesson, in engineer-days; the arithmetic was computed by running code):

| Task | O | M | P | E | s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| schema + migration | 1 | 2 | 5 | 2.33 | 0.67 |
| API endpoints | 2 | 4 | 9 | 4.50 | 1.17 |
| auth integration | 1 | 3 | 8 | 3.50 | 1.17 |
| tests | 2 | 3 | 6 | 3.33 | 0.67 |
| deploy + runbook | 1 | 2 | 4 | 2.17 | 0.50 |
| **Total** | 7 | 14 | 32 | **15.83** | **1.96** (if independent) |

Observe: the sum of most-likely values is 14, the expected total is 15.8, and the sum of pessimistic values is 32. Quoting 14 days would be a coin-flip-or-worse promise. A reasonable statement: "About 16 engineer-days of effort; I would plan for 20 (expected plus two formula standard deviations is 19.8) and I would not be surprised by more if the auth integration is harder than assumed." Converting effort-days into calendar time requires dividing by your real availability, which is rarely 100%.

### 3.5 Root-cause analysis and blameless postmortem

An incident review aims at a system that is harder to break, not a culprit. "Blameless" does not mean accountability-free; it means we assume people acted reasonably given what they knew, and we ask why that was a reasonable thing to do and what made the failure possible.

Procedure:

1. **Stabilise and preserve evidence** (logs, metrics, config versions) before they rotate.
2. **Build a timeline** in a single time zone with sources: what happened, when detected, by what (alert/user), actions taken and their effect. Separate observations from interpretations.
3. **Impact**: who, how long, what data or money; measured, with the method stated.
4. **Causal analysis**: ask "why" repeatedly, but expect several branches, not one chain. Distinguish *trigger* (what started it), *root causes / contributing factors* (conditions that allowed it: missing validation, absent alert, unclear ownership, risky default), and *detection and recovery* factors (why it took N minutes to notice and M to fix).
5. **Verify the root cause**: a hypothesis is not a cause until you reproduce it, or show the failure vanishes when the cause is removed, or at least predict something unobserved that you then observe. Say how confident you are.
6. **Actions**: prevent, detect, mitigate. Each action is specific, has an owner and a due date, and a way to check it is done. "Be more careful" is not an action. Prefer actions that change the system (a test, a guard, an alert, an automation) over actions that change people's memory.
7. **What went well** (detection, communication, rollback) and **where we got lucky**.
8. Share it and follow up on the actions.

Template:

```
Postmortem: <title>   Date of incident / authors / status
Summary (3 sentences) / Impact (measured) / Detection (how, how long)
Timeline (UTC) / Root cause(s) and contributing factors / Trigger
How we verified the cause / Resolution and recovery
What went well / Where we got lucky / What went poorly
Action items: [type: prevent|detect|mitigate] [owner] [due] [verification]
Lessons / open questions
```

Anti-patterns: a single "human error" root cause; stopping the "why" chain at a person; all actions being "add documentation"; unverified causes; hindsight bias.

### 3.6 Performance report

Content: question; workload and why it is representative; environment (hardware, versions, config, data size); method (warm-up, repetitions, tool, exclusions); results with variance and units; analysis with profile evidence; limits; recommendation against a decision threshold stated in advance. Never report numbers you did not measure or compare across machines. 4.4 gives the method; this is the report it produces.

### 3.7 Security review

A review of a design or change asking what could go wrong, defensively. Structure: assets and data classes; trust boundaries and entry points (diagram); actors and their capabilities (including legitimate users abusing features); threats per boundary (a simple prompt list: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege, known as STRIDE); existing controls; gaps with severity and a proposed fix or accepted-risk sign-off with an owner; secrets, logging of sensitive data, retention, dependencies; abuse limits (rate, size, quotas); test evidence (negative tests for authorisation). Output: a decision (approve / approve with conditions / reject) and tracked actions. Defensive only; do not attack systems you do not own.

### 3.8 Migration plan

For data, schema, service or provider changes. Components: goal and success criteria; inventory of what moves and what depends on it; **phases** designed so each is independently reversible until the point of no return (expand, migrate/backfill, dual-write or shadow-read, verify, switch, contract/cleanup); **verification** at each phase (row counts, checksums, sampled comparisons, shadow comparison of responses); **rollback** with a tested procedure and a time limit after which it is impossible; **point of no return** named explicitly; communication and freeze windows; performance and locking impact on production; monitoring and abort criteria written before starting; ownership and schedule. Test the migration on a production-like copy and measure its duration there (do not extrapolate from a small dataset).

### 3.9 Post-implementation review

Weeks after release: outcome versus goals (did the metric move?), estimate versus actual with reasons, what the design got wrong, operational cost, incidents. It feeds estimation calibration and ADR revisit triggers.

### 3.10 Technical defense format

A timed oral examination of your own design: you present, others challenge. Format (20 minutes):

| Segment | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Problem and constraints | 3 min | Requirement, ambiguities resolved, non-goals |
| Design | 5 min | Diagram, data flow, key decisions with alternatives |
| Risks and verification | 4 min | Failure modes, tests, evidence you have |
| Challenge round | 6 min | Questions from reviewers; no slides |
| Close | 2 min | Open questions, what you would do next, what you do not know |

Defence rules: answer the question asked; give evidence or say you do not have it; distinguish what you measured from what you believe; concede valid objections and state what changes; do not defend a decision on the grounds that you already made it. See the rubric in 9 (reasoning-quality grading).

## 4. Worked example

### 4.1 From request to clarified requirement

Request: "Customers should be able to export their invoices."

Restatement: "An authenticated customer can obtain a file of their own invoices for a chosen period."

Top questions ranked by cost of being wrong (with proposed defaults):

1. Who can export: only the customer, or also staff on their behalf? *Default: customer only; staff out of scope.* (Access control: highest cost.)
2. Volume: largest customer's invoice count? *Default: design for 100k, check the real maximum in production data.* (Drives sync vs async.)
3. Format: CSV, PDF, both? Fields? *Default: CSV, fixed column list reviewed with finance.*
4. Delivery: immediate download or emailed link? Retention of generated files? *Default: async job, signed link expiring in 24 h.*
5. Personal data: do invoices contain data that must be redacted or logged on access? *Default: audit log of exports; ask privacy contact.*

Assumptions recorded with owners and dates; non-goals: PDF rendering, bulk export across customers.

### 4.2 An ADR from the clarified design

```
ADR-007: Generate invoice exports asynchronously
Status: Accepted    Date: <date>
Context: Largest customer may have up to 100k invoices (assumption A2, unconfirmed). A synchronous request
  that streams 100k rows risks proxy and client timeouts (timeouts: see ops). Team already runs a job
  queue for reports. Exports contain personal data, so storage and expiry matter.
Decision: We will create exports in a background job, store the file in private object storage,
  and give the customer a time-limited signed link (24 h).
Alternatives:
  - Synchronous streaming response: simplest, no storage; rejected because failure and timeout behaviour
    at 100k rows is unmeasured and retries would re-run the query.
  - Email attachment: rejected; personal data in email, size limits.
Consequences: + no timeout coupling, retryable, auditable. - needs job status endpoint, storage lifecycle
  rule, cleanup. Harder: debugging requires job logs.
Revisit when: measured p95 generation time for the largest customer is under the request timeout with margin
  on production-like data (then reconsider sync), or when invoice volume grows 10x.
```

Note how the ADR exposes its own weakest premise (A2, the volume) and says what would change the decision.

### 4.3 Reasoning-quality grading in action (a weak vs strong risk statement)

Weak: "Risk: performance. Mitigation: optimise."
Strong: "Risk R3: the export query scans `invoices` for customers with >50k rows (assumption: index on `customer_id` exists, unverified; check with `EXPLAIN` on a production-sized copy). If wrong, a single export could hold a long read for minutes and delay the replica. Mitigation: verify the index before build; run exports on the read replica with a statement timeout; abort criterion: any export over 5 minutes in staging blocks release."

The strong version names a mechanism, an unverified assumption, a check, a consequence and an abort criterion (the numbers in it are placeholders to be replaced by your measurements).

### 4.4 A postmortem excerpt (simulated incident)

Simulated, for teaching; not a real event.

*Summary:* A deployment at 14:05 UTC changed the export job to read from the replica; at 14:20 the replica lagged 40 minutes behind and customers downloaded exports missing the newest invoices. Detected at 16:40 by a customer ticket.

*Trigger:* the deployment. *Contributing factors:* (1) no lag alert on the replica; (2) no test that compared export contents with the primary; (3) the ADR assumed lag was negligible and had no stated revisit condition; (4) ticket triage routed the report to billing, not engineering, adding 90 minutes.

*Verification of cause:* reproduced in staging by pausing replication for 10 minutes and generating an export (missing the last invoices); switching the read to the primary removed the symptom.

*Actions:* [detect] alert when replica lag exceeds a threshold agreed with the data owner (owner: platform, due date, check: alert fires in a test); [prevent] consistency test comparing export row count to the primary for a seeded dataset in CI; [mitigate] export job records the replica's reported position and refuses to run above a lag limit; [process] triage rule routing "missing data" tickets to on-call. Where we got lucky: no customer acted on the stale data before the fix.

Notice what is absent: no name, no "should have known", every action testable.

## 5. Production perspective

**What a senior engineer does differently.** Writes the design doc to find disagreement early and welcomes the strongest objection; makes assumptions visible and cheap to check; chooses boring technology unless a requirement forbids it; records the decision and its revisit trigger; gives ranges, not points; writes postmortems that change systems; treats a migration as a product with its own tests and rollback.

**Maintainability.** Link ADRs from code and README, put status at the top of each design doc, mark superseded documents, and keep the runbook beside the code.

**Operations.** Every design states metrics, alerts and runbook; an action that adds an alert also adds the runbook line for when it fires.

**Process.** Use the lightest process that gives review; adapt to team standards rather than imposing heavy process.

**Common beginner mistakes**

| Mistake | How to recognise | Diagnosis/fix |
|---|---|---|
| Alternatives that are strawmen | The rejected options fail for trivial reasons | Ask a colleague to argue for the alternative |
| Design doc as a diary of the implementation | Written after coding, no open questions | Write before coding; list decisions needed |
| Single-number estimate | "Three days" with no range or assumptions | Three-point with exclusions |
| Root cause = a person | "Engineer deployed without checking" | Ask what made it possible and easy |
| Unmeasured performance claims | "Should be fast" | Method + measurement (4.4) |
| Migration with no rollback | Plan ends at "run script" | Phases, verification, point of no return |
| Security review as checklist | All boxes ticked, no threats named | Boundary diagram, abuse cases, negative tests |

## 6. Failure laboratory

**The flawed postmortem.** Read this (invented) postmortem excerpt and critique it before reading the reveal.

```
Incident: Orders API returned 500s for 35 minutes.
Root cause: Developer J pushed a config change without testing it.
Why: He was rushing because of the deadline.
Fix: Developers must test config changes before pushing. Add a reminder to the wiki.
Timeline: 14:10 config pushed. 14:45 fixed.
Impact: Some customers could not order.
Lessons: Be more careful.
```

**Predict first:** list at least six problems with this document; say which are about content, which about blame, and which about verifiability; say what the "Fix" would change in the system (hint: what changes in the code, pipeline or alerts?).

### Reveal (read after predicting)

Problems, grouped:

- **Blame and shallow causation:** names a person as root cause; "rushing" is speculation about motive; the analysis stops at a human act. Questions it never asks: why can a config change reach production untested? Why does a bad config cause 500s rather than a rejected deploy? Why did it take 35 minutes? How was it detected?
- **No verification of cause:** no evidence the config caused the errors (logs, diff, reproduction). It could be a coincidence with a dependency failing.
- **Weak impact:** "Some customers", no count, no duration measurement method, no data impact or revenue statement.
- **Timeline gaps:** no detection time or mechanism, no escalation steps, no mitigation actions, no time zone.
- **Non-actions:** "Developers must test" and "reminder in the wiki" change only memory; no owner, due date, or verification. System changes it should have considered: config validation in CI, staged rollout/canary, automatic rollback on error-rate increase, a config schema, an alert on 5xx rate with a time-to-detect target.
- **No contributing factors, no "went well / got lucky".**

Rewrite procedure (systematic): (1) collect evidence (deploy log, config diff, error-rate graph); (2) rebuild timeline with sources; (3) separate trigger from contributing factors, and detection and recovery; (4) test the causal claim (revert the config in staging reproduces/removes the errors); (5) write actions of type prevent/detect/mitigate with owner, date, check; (6) review it with the people involved for factual accuracy. A model rewrite appears in the solutions file.

## 7. Retrieval practice

Answers in the solutions file.

1. Give the steps for turning "make the app faster" into a clarified requirement. Which two questions come first and why?
2. Why include "do nothing" among the alternatives?
3. What belongs in an ADR's "Context" and what does not?
4. When and how do you supersede an ADR? Why not edit it?
5. For tasks with (O, M, P) = (1, 3, 8) and (2, 3, 6) days, compute E and s for each and for the sum (assuming independence). Name one reason the combined spread is understated.
6. A colleague estimates "two weeks". List four questions you ask.
7. Distinguish trigger, root cause and contributing factor with an example.
8. Why must a postmortem verify the root cause, and give two ways to do it?
9. Rewrite the action "be more careful with migrations" as a prevent, a detect and a mitigate action.
10. List what a performance report must contain that a table of timings does not.
11. Name the point of no return in a typical expand/migrate/contract schema change, and what must be true before crossing it.
12. In a technical defense you are asked something you do not know. What do you say, and what do you avoid?

## 8. Independent implementation challenge

**Mode A.** In 60 minutes, closed book, write a one-page ADR **and** a three-point estimator.

(a) ADR for this decision: "Store money amounts as integer minor units (cents) vs `Decimal` columns vs floats" in a service you invent in two sentences. Include context with at least three forces, two real alternatives, consequences, and a revisit trigger.

(b) `estimate.py` (Python 3.12, stdlib only): `estimate(tasks: list[tuple[str, float, float, float]]) -> dict` returning per-task expected value and standard deviation, total expected, total standard deviation under independence, and the sum of pessimistic values. Validate `0 <= O <= M <= P` and non-empty input (`ValueError`). Tests: the table in section 3.4 (expected total 15.83 to 2 decimals), invalid ordering, single task. State in a comment why the independence assumption makes the spread a lower bound.

### Reasoning-quality rubric (use for every document in this lesson)

Score each dimension 0-4 (0 absent, 1 superficial, 2 adequate, 3 strong, 4 exemplary), then weight per field 12 above.

| Dimension | 1 (superficial) | 3 (strong) |
|---|---|---|
| Problem framing | Restates the request | States goals, non-goals, success measures |
| Evidence | Assertions | Measurements, citations, reproductions; labels assumptions |
| Alternatives | Strawmen | Credible options with conditions under which each wins |
| Trade-offs | Lists pros only | Names what is given up and who bears it |
| Risk | Generic ("performance") | Mechanism, probability/impact reasoning, check, mitigation, abort criterion |
| Verifiability | Unfalsifiable plans | Testable acceptance criteria, rollback tested |
| Honesty about uncertainty | Confident throughout | Distinguishes known, believed, unknown; says how to find out |
| Fit | Ignores team standards/existing system | Works with existing architecture and conventions |
| Communication | Dense or vague | Skimmable, ordered by importance, unambiguous |

Defense rubric (15 points): answers the question asked (3); evidence or honest uncertainty (4); concedes valid objections and states the consequence (3); consistent with documents (2); time management (1); calm, specific language (2).

## 11. Mastery gate

Pass = 80/100 with no critical failure. Critical failures: (1) postmortem names an individual as the cause or has no verification or no testable actions; (2) invented facts presented as facts (organisational data, measurements); (3) design with no alternatives or no rollback for an irreversible change; (4) estimate as a single number without exclusions; (5) security review with no concrete threats; (6) defense answers that assert unmeasured claims as fact when challenged.

Evidence: all deliverables and the recording; a live 10-minute challenge round with a reviewer (Mode A).

If failed: documents -> rewrite the weakest two with a peer's critique logged; postmortem -> take the flawed postmortem in 6, produce the full rewrite with a reproduction script; estimation -> estimate three tasks you already finished, compare to actuals, and write a calibration note; defense -> repeat with a different set of reviewers.
